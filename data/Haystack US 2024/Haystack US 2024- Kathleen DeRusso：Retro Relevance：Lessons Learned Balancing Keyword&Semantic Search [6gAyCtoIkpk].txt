Quick refresher on text search: this is the text search we all know and love. You're taking text, translating it, parsing it, turning it into keywords, analyzing it, creating inverted document lists—this is how tech search has worked for decades. There are tried and tested algorithms such as TF-IDF and BM25. Starting with TF-IDF, term frequency over inverse document frequency shows how important a keyword in a document or a corpus is, heavily used from 1972. When The Godfather was introduced in theaters, VCRs were introduced, and Pong was an arcade game. After TF-IDF, we moved to BM25 in 1994 with advanced improvements like document length normalization and frequency saturation. BM25 is heavily used today, synonymously known as tech search. While it pains me to think of the '90s as retro, BM25 is as old as Forrest Gump or when we were collecting Beanie Babies. It’s been around for a while.

This technology comes with a robust toolbox—analyzers and tokenizers pre-process text before searching. You have functionalities like whitespace tokenization, punctuation handling, stop-word removal (not as used today), case normalization, stemming, and lemmatization. Filters, field weighting, and boosts allow for structured data filtration, isolating specific content like author, genre, or publication date. Boosts go from titles being more important than descriptions to sophisticated ones like geo-distance or popularity.

Re-ranking strategies such as learning to rank, personalization, contextual re-ranking, and semantic re-ranking are essential. Synonyms for slang and domain-specific jargon are crucial for semantic search models that might not handle them well. Business rules encompass many custom rules, and some common use cases include ensuring diverse result sets or targeting ads based on query results or personalization factors. While not always aligned with relevance, combining business rules with relevance is necessary—you know, they pay the bills and might be contractually obligatory.

Quick refresher on semantic search: we take text strings, translate them into embedding models as arrays of floating-point numbers (dense vectors), and search on these using approximate nearest neighbor (ANN) or brute-force KNN searches. Alternately, we use learned sparse retriever models to expand original text. Vectors can be utilized beyond traditional search retrieval, including personalization or summarization. They are useful for scoring or filtering, aligning with semantic and final re-ranking. Reranking is invaluable when models don't immediately return the right answer, providing a critical tool, especially when not getting the top result.
