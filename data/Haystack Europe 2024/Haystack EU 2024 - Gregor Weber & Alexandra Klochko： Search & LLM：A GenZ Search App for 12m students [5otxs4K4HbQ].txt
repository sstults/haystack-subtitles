**Alexandra**: I wanted to show you the complexity of working with various types of PDFs. Our backend infrastructure processes student-uploaded content via OCR to extract transcripts, and machine learning models provide additional document insights. We store text embeddings from OpenAI in DB and stream events from our frontend into BigQuery. **Gregor**: Switching over to our journey with large language models in search, we jumped on using LLMs early on, even before ChatGPT, due to the potential for handling unstructured content. Our first attempt was extracting keywords from documents, which were tricky due to OCR errors and noise. We used GPT to extract keywords and used them to improve search recall for less common terms not in titles or descriptions.

Testing search is challenging without purchase events, so we focus on meaningful user events, such as spending 120 seconds on content, indicating a positive interaction. Our A/B tests evaluate search-to-study event rates and retention impact.

A core learning was GPT-3 was costly and not as effective, but models have improved significantly.

**Alexandra**: We also explored Google-style follow-up questions for users to engage with relevant content. Students often struggle to phrase good search terms, so we aimed to provide more intuitive interaction, even though the quality of language models back then was not the best.

Later iterations included introducing an AI chat-like companion in search for formulating better search terms or summarizing content, leveraging cues on user inactivity to suggest the AI feature. Unfortunately, it initially lacked visibility and didn't engage users.

In subsequent design attempts, we included AI answers directly in search results, with AI summaries but faced latency issues impacting user experience. We noticed users prefer full study notes over summarized answers, leading to evaluation data showing no positive impact.

**Gregor**: We also delved into backend improvements, learning to rank content by quality using features like readability and structuredness. This approach improved content ranking without relying solely on user interactions. LLMs facilitated training classification models based on GPT-4 output, distinguishing well-structured, readable content from lower-quality submissions. Despite OCR limitations with handwritten content, leveraging GPT-4 Vision improved transcripts' accuracy and usability, though cost remains a barrier for large-scale processing due to pricing.

**Alexandra**: For our "Did You Mean?" feature, we attempted using smaller LLMs, but found balancing speed and quality difficult, with less satisfactory results in languages besides English and German.

Another initiative involved building a knowledge graph to support continuous learning by mapping related topics from our content. This process of identifying key topics and entities from search data required significant duplication and disambiguation work with LLMs. It demonstrated LLMs' prolific creativity but necessitated stringent refinement.

**Gregor**: Lastly, an attempt to create a comprehensive AI results page merging AI conversation features with search showed strong user engagement with AI chats. However, latency and the persistent user preference for full content over AI summaries remained challenges.

Summarizing our learnings: LLMs facilitate fast MVP development, especially indexing time tasks. They enhance machine learning tasks efficiency, but cost, speed, and quality trade-offs persist. Non-LLM improvements also play crucial roles, with lessons emphasizing understanding user needs over leveraging flashy tech for its own sake.
