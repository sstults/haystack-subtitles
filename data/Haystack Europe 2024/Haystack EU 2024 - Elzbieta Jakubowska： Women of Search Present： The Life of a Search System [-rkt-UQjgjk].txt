A word about the evolution of search, in a chronological way. In the last 24 years, many things have changed in our industry. Pre-2000, we were focused on pattern search and exact search. Then in 2000, text analysis became important. In 2010, natural language processing emerged. These are just ballpark figures, so don't keep us fixed on the exact dates. First, we will look into search in the grand scheme of things, then how things usually evolve, some adjustments to improve the state of things, up-leveling, and a few pieces of advice from us.

In the beginning, you are just here: you have a data store, a bunch of unstructured data, structured documents, images, audio files, streaming data, and probably much, much more. But you want to get here: you want to have a secure fancy search system with fancy index pipelines, low latency, a beautiful UI, and a happy user in the end. How to get there?

Okay, firstly, we begin with a typical story of building a search system. Things are rolling. When you are new to the topic, this is a little bit of a storytelling part, and I’ll be reading a lot of notes because it is easier for me, and I don't want to ruin it for you, so prepare.

How it usually starts: first, you take your data and push it somehow to the search engine of your choice, probably in an offline batch mode, on-demand, or on a schedule. Then you build some backend search logic and expose it via search API to query data. The next step is building a fancy or rudimental UI. Soon enough, you will notice that data processing becomes a bottleneck. The volume grows and becomes so enormous that it pushes you into constant write operation mode, so you'll scale up and scale out the entire platform. The money counter is ticking.

Product requirements force you to switch from offline processing to online, so you build a new data processing platform that adds even more write pressure. The money counter goes up. You realize that probably not all changes should trigger an update, and not all of them are equally important, so you add checks, delta checks, and priority concepts. Somewhere during that process, you notice that data quality is not what you expected. You invest in data enrichment and data augmentation, the focus is on adding more signals to existing data, the more the better, right? The AI and all the big guns are pulled out. The money counter doubles its speed.

Finally, the data quality becomes a hot topic in your team; content sanitization and deduplication are implemented. Sadly, only now, and someday there is a doomsday: the search engine crashes on you and you lose your data. The search engine was an authoritative source of truth. There were no recent backups, and the pipeline from the source takes three days. Not a good situation.

The quality of search results bothers you all along. The simple user tracking tool you put in place for measuring KPIs shows low CTR, low success metric, or low engagement. You can point out some obvious problems with results. Tempted by an easy fix, you apply the hack into the search API or directly into the search engine query, but that does not scale and you know it. So you start collecting underperforming queries in a spreadsheet. There are too many to keep fixing them in the codebase independently, and the outcome is hard to predict. Your approach evolves: you build your own or use existing tools to manage your queries and override or tune the system's default behavior. This can quickly get out of control, and you end up with two parallel search systems: a back-office system and a default search that may override each other.

Following trends and mainstream media, you are tempted to incorporate new technologies: you integrate with AI services, hoping for better query understanding, query enrichment, classification; you build them yourself in a more risky approach instead of using integrations. 

Anyway, spending money reaches the next level; the desperation level hits new highs. Despite all that time and effort, the return on investment stays low while spending money gets insanely high. You start realizing that the changes you need are more fundamental and technology-dependent. Intrigued or forced, you start exploring how the search engine of your choice actually works and how to operate and tune it. You discover a variety of search functions and their usage.

At some point, you read that pushing data efficiently into a search engine is insufficient; it needs proper modeling, aka design for search, and here congratulations, you have started connecting the dots and experimenting with different mappings and search logic. Your time investment results in your very first search strategy—it could be a greedy algorithm with small boosts on specific fields or a multi-stage pipeline. It doesn't matter; you are proud of it as it clearly fixes the problems at hand. Full of hope, you deploy it to production waiting for a big breakthrough, which doesn't happen, and instead, you have unwanted side effects. Finally, you get trapped in a cycle of failing attempts.

Our story continues on another slide. This is a long journey. After a few dozen attempts, you notice a gap between your test queries and production. To be more successful, you are missing your actual user's queries. So you invest in building a query log. If you are lucky, queries from production are close enough to the ones you tuned for. If not, you need to start again with your improvements—it's an iterative process. After a few iterations like that, you probably suffer from long cycles—your goal now is to confirm progress or at least prevent regression quickly. So you invest in a better test environment where your ideas can be validated quickly before they hit production.

What is missing here? That's the validation set against which you can predict better outcomes of your improvements, and some way of measuring or quantifying the change. Luckily, you quickly realize that data is already available—that's the user tracking you have for KPIs. Probably some important details are missing in that data, for example, filter usage, returned document IDs, or sequence of actions not combined into a session. Other essential components of this lab include a golden set of queries and judgment lists. All those mechanics make you faster but not necessarily more successful. Now, let's take a look at possible adjustments to what we have seen. Where can you apply a few adjustments just to improve the state, not dramatically change it, but improve it?

**Ella:**  
On data quality and storage: Centralize all in-flight processes and focus on producing a custodial data warehouse. What are the benefits of it? Own the data flowing through your system. It's an authoritative source of truth. Changes are being tracked; you know who changed what, when it happened. Standardized data with unified quality standards. This can be your answer to indexing the same information multiple times from different sources, each with a different quality. Control the rate and throughput of your writes; don't be reactive. Perform costly operations only once on the standardized data.

On tracking user journeys: Invest in tracking users before and after their search sessions; join events in a search session. This is a high-effort but also very high return investment. Use data to understand users and prioritize fixes. Define a universal schema for events. Use it for personalization and machine learning tasks. Use that data to measure offline relevance.

On building and testing frameworks: Create frameworks for offline and online experimentations; build both of them or at least one. Also, create a golden set of queries to measure your gains. For e-commerce, these can be 'iPhone' queries; never break the 'iPhone' queries. Sor, yes, manual data set labeling still matters. Standardize analysis for easier comparison of experiment results. Choose the right offline metric or metrics and monitor alignment between offline and online metrics.

On manual curation and override systems:  Occasionally override the default behavior of your system. However, use it sparingly; it's just a temporary fix. It's most useful when users exhibit brand new search behavior and in specialized domains like medical fields. It can really save your day.

On strategy at some point: Split retrieval from the whole search strategy. Documents designed for search need to support your search strategy. Model your data as you will be searching in it. Have a fallback when the fancy things need an emergency shutdown because sometimes they do.

On ranking strategy improvements: Speed ranking is the second sequential stream. Rate candidates based on business values and quality checks first. Apply your own personalization data points in the search tracking before you try using third-party services. Learning to rank is great; try first with an offline approach before going online, which is more costly.

Level up: When are you ready to level up your search and bring it to the next level? Do you have lots of unstructured data? A pretty good end-to-end search system in place already? Enough engineers to green-field a new project? A sizable budget for vector search databases and LLMs? If your answers are yes, you might be ready for Gen. How can you use it to your advantage and enrich your existing search results with semantic results? Build a rack chat interface, include rack-based signals, generate "Did you mean" candidates, augment spell check, and generate keywords metadata.

On building a knowledge graph: Do you have data with inherent hierarchical relationships like e-commerce categories or specialized vocabularies? Do you have specialists that can help with the information science aspects? If your answers are yes, you might be ready to build a knowledge graph and use it to disambiguate user intent and increase recall by pulling overarching concepts. 

Our advice: Introducing need-driven development (NDD) — build what you need, as you need it: no sooner, no later. Start small and simple, pick the easiest tool for the job to get you started now. Try to max out your technology before migrating to something else; technology is just a medium toward your goal. Read, research, exercise questions, and options. 

Don't forget: Measure something—anything that you can benchmark against. Use what works for you. Limit entropy of your technology stack. Don't take shortcuts on data; it won’t pay off. Don't overstate the impact of new technology but don't be ignorant. Don't build rigid systems; search is not a coliseum. Remember need-driven development.
