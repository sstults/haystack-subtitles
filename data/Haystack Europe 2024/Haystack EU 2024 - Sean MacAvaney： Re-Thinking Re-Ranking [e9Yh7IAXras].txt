Typically, a reranking system would look like this: first, you have some user query where the user enters “tablet” or something into the search engine. You run your retriever, maybe that’s BM25 for lexical search or fancy or vector methods, to get some list of results over your dataset. This is an okay ranking, but maybe not so great all the time, right? Especially if you’re doing something like lexical search, there can be a lot of problems in how these results are ranked.

Now there is a problem with this process. If you miss a document when doing retrieving and you miss this golden document that doesn’t quite meet the filter, you lose it completely. You’ll never have it down here to rerank. People come up with all sorts of great approaches like learned sparse methods, hybrid methods, etc., to enhance this pool of documents that the reranker gets, so you don’t miss what you’re really looking for.

What I would argue is that what we wish we could do is run the ranker over the entire corpus. We have millions of documents and really trust models like GPT’s assessment of search result quality. We wish we could score every single document to ensure we get that gold document at the top, assuming it's the best document and your ranker can identify that. Of course, this is just way too costly, doing all this inference takes a lot of time, and using GPT incurs a lot of API credits and so on.

The next best option, I would argue, is to give the ranker access not just to the list of initial search results you get from your retriever but also to the whole rest of the dataset, the whole corpus. Now this seems like the same thing as scoring everything, but leveraging this initial set of results is super powerful to unlock a lot of other documents in your corpus. It seems crazy, but I'll show you how this can be done with minimal cost. Basically, with essentially no extra cost, it can not just improve reranking methods using GPT or whatever but retrievers like ColBERT and others. The way you do this is first you just make a guess. You don't really know anything about your opponent’s board initially. You make a guess, ask your opponent if it’s a hit. It’s as if you’re launching a strike into the ocean, though you don’t actually have any intelligence about where the ships really are, but let's ignore that limitation of the game. What does this have to do with ranking? I would argue that when you’re doing reranking you essentially think of these initial list of results you get as reasonable guesses on this board. Instead of asking your opponent if your document is a hit or a miss, you’re asking your ranker, “Is this a good document? Is this a bad document? I would need to keep on guessing randomly until I found all of them. We've learned a lot of information about what makes a document relevant to this query, by identifying a document that is considered relevant by a strong model like GPT.

Adaptive reranking is this approach where we leverage this information from high-scoring documents to find other ones missed by the retriever. We don’t need these documents to come back in that first set of guesses. We just need something that gets roughly close to allow us to explore the area around it. Maybe we send these four other documents adjacent to our fancy reranking method. Some of them are not so good. The next question is how do we decide which documents are worth checking? In Battleship, you know characteristics about the shapes of the ships, like their lengths and they must be oriented in specific ways on the game board. In ranking, we don’t have that exactly, but we have something similar. One option, once we identify a document we think is good, is to issue that same document as a query back to our engine and take the top results for that query. We treat this document as a query and find the most similar documents to it. If this is a good document, documents similar to it are probably also good. This goes to a lot of IR theory from Van Rijsbergen with the cluster hypothesis and so on.

In a practical sense, the idea is that a similar document to one you think is good might also be good. We don’t know for sure, but we’ll use the ranker to confirm if it’s actually good. This is a really bad idea because it’s really slow. You don’t want to actually execute a lookup for your document, maybe even encode it as a new query, execute it against the engine, all that.
